# EPA Climate Change Indicators: Heat-Related Deaths (Technical Documentation)

- **URL:** https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-06/heat-deaths_documentation.pdf
- **Accessed:** 2026-07-31
- **Region:** United States
- **Type:** dataset

EPA technical documentation (updated June 2024) for its "Heat-Related
Deaths" climate change indicator, built from CDC National Vital Statistics
System (NVSS) death-certificate data queried through CDC WONDER, plus the
CDC-coordinated National Environmental Public Health Tracking (EPHT)
Program.

## Key facts

- Scope: "the rate of U.S. annual deaths between 1979 and 2022 for which
  heat was classified on death certificates as the underlying (direct)
  cause," and separately "the rate of U.S. summer deaths between 1999 and
  2021 for which heat was classified as either the underlying cause or a
  contributing factor."
- Undercounting, national estimate: "some statistical approaches estimate
  that more than 1,300 deaths per year in the United States are due to
  extreme heat, compared with about 750 deaths per year in the 'underlying
  and contributing causes' data set" that CDC/EPA officially track — i.e.
  the official count is roughly half the modeled true toll.
  (Sarofim et al., 2016, as cited by EPA.)
- Why the undercount: "many deaths associated with extreme heat are not
  identified as such by the medical examiner and might not be properly
  coded on the death certificate. In many cases, they might just classify
  the cause of death as a cardiovascular or respiratory disease."
- 1995 Chicago heat wave, illustrative case: researchers found "692 deaths
  in excess of the background death rate" for the Chicago area between
  June 21 and August 10, 1995 (reported as "approximately 700" for Cook
  County, July 11–27), while "only 465 deaths were attributed to extreme
  heat on death certificates during that period" — official certificates
  captured about two-thirds of the modeled excess.
- Response measures work: after a 1995 heat wave, Milwaukee built a
  heat-emergency response plan, and "during a 1999 heat wave, this plan cut
  heat-related deaths nearly in half compared with what was expected"
  (Weisskopf et al., 2002).
- Demographic disparity: EPA's Figure 2 tracks heat-linked cardiovascular
  deaths for three groups — the general population, adults 65+, and
  non-Hispanic Black people — and the documentation states "non-Hispanic
  Black people have a death rate higher than the general population
  average."
- Data limitation stated by EPA itself: "this indicator very likely
  underestimates the number of deaths caused by exposure to heat,"
  since ICD codes only capture heat as an explicit cause, not as a
  contributing stressor to a heart attack or respiratory death.

## Summary

EPA's own technical documentation for its national heat-death indicator
shows the official U.S. death-certificate count (roughly 750/year) is
about half of independent statistical estimates of the true toll (1,300+/
year), with the 1995 Chicago heat wave as a concrete case where certificates
(465) undercounted modeled excess deaths (~700). It also gives one of the
few citable examples of a response measure with a measured outcome:
Milwaukee's post-1995 heat emergency plan cut deaths "nearly in half" in
its first real test in 1999.
